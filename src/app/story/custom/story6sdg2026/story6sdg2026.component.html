In 2017, Sierra Leone could not tell the world how clean its rivers were. It had no way to monitor freshwater quality, so the box for SDG indicator 6.3.2 stayed empty. That mattered for drinking water, farms, and the people living downstream of artisanal gold mines.

By 2025, things had changed. Sierra Leone had built a national water-quality monitoring programme. Local communities now help monitor rivers and contribute to national SDG reporting. In the Taia-Pampana basin, citizen scientists are measuring heavy-metal pollution. The UN now calls Sierra Leone a global pioneer in combining citizen-generated data with official statistics, and six African countries are using its model as a blueprint.

It is a small story. It is also a clear picture of where the Sustainable Development Goals stand in late 2026, four years before the deadline. Progress happens when people, money, and good data line up. Right now, all three are under pressure.

The scorecard: real gains, too slow

The UN’s Sustainable Development Goals Report 2026, published in July 2026, looked at 139 targets with enough data to judge a trend. Put the two groups that are still moving together and 36% are on track or progressing moderately. 49% are moving too slowly. 15% are now worse than in 2015.

None of the gender-equality targets are on track. The independent Sustainable Development Report 2026, from the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network, is blunter still. It projects that only about 16% of targets will actually be reached by 2030. That is a different method from the UN’s 36%, and the two numbers should not be mixed.

Dig deeper: how the 36% and the 16% are counted
  • The UN report of 7 July 2026 assesses 139 targets with trend data. 15% are on track or already met. 21% show moderate progress. Together, that is the 36%.
  • 32% show only marginal progress and 17% are stagnant. The story reports those together as 49% moving too slowly. 15% have fallen below the 2015 baseline.
  • The report’s key messages say none of the gender-equality targets are on track.
  • SDSN, on 23 June 2026, projects that about 16% of targets will be achieved by 2030. It is a forecast of what will be met, not a reading of today’s pace. Keep it next to the UN figure, not in place of it.

What a decade of work changed

The past decade was not wasted. Since 2015, the same UN report records gains that are easy to miss when the headline is “off track”:

  • Nearly 1 billion more people have safely managed drinking water
  • 1.2 billion 1.2 billion more people have safe sanitation
  • 92% 92 percent of humanity now has electricity
  • 74% 74 percent use the internet, up from 40% in 2015
  • Over half the world has some form of social protection, a first
  • About a third fewer new HIV infections than in 2015

What is stuck is just as concrete.

  • 1 in 10 people still live in extreme poverty
  • 2.3 billion people face food insecurity, more than in 2015
  • 150 million+ children are stunted
  • 118 million people have been forced from their homes by conflict
Dig deeper: the gains, and what they do not yet cover
  • About a third of the world’s electricity is renewable. New HIV infections fell 30% from 2015 to 2024, and AIDS deaths fell 35%.
  • 134 countries have met the child-mortality target. 59 have eliminated at least one neglected tropical disease. Disaster deaths are down 65% compared with the previous decade.
  • 2.1 billion people still lack safely managed drinking water. The billion who gained it are not the whole story.
  • Maternal mortality is about three times the global target. One in five people experience discrimination. Temperatures in 2025 were 1.43°C above pre-industrial levels.

All of these figures are from the UN report and its July 2026 key messages.

Follow the money

The biggest headwind is financial. According to the OECD, official development assistance fell 23.1% in 2025. That is the largest drop ever recorded, and it takes aid back to where it was when the SDGs began. The United States accounted for three-quarters of the cut. The OECD expects aid to fall again in 2026.

23.1% 23.1 percent

The 2025 fall in official development assistance, in real terms. The largest drop on record. OECD, 9 April 2026.

Meanwhile, developing countries face an SDG financing gap of roughly $4 trillion a year. Governments agreed a plan to tackle it, the Sevilla Commitment, at the 2025 Financing for Development conference. It covers debt relief, stronger development banks, and better domestic tax systems. At July’s UN High-level Political Forum, countries reaffirmed it. They also clashed openly over climate language. The result is commitment on paper and scarcity in practice.

Dig deeper: the aid drop, and the Sevilla Commitment
  • DAC aid was USD 174.3 billion in 2025, down 23.1% in real terms, back to the 2015 level.
  • The United States accounted for 75% of the fall. Its own aid fell 56.9%. Germany became the largest donor for the first time.
  • Core funding to the UN fell 27%. Humanitarian aid fell 35.8%. The OECD projects a further 5.8% decline in 2026.
  • The Sevilla Commitment, agreed from 30 June to 3 July 2025, targets that annual gap of about $4 trillion.
  • At the High-level Political Forum on 15 July 2026, a recorded vote of 82 to 54, with 12 abstentions, removed a reference to the UAE Consensus, the COP28 energy-transition language. The EU, speaking through Ireland, said it was disappointed.

Is Europe following up?

Yes, with an asterisk.

On the numbers, Eurostat’s 2026 monitoring report shows the EU improving on most goals. It made strong progress on decent work, gender equality, education, inequality, and responsible consumption. It is moving backwards on clean water (SDG 6) and life on land (SDG 15), and it has made no progress on global partnerships (SDG 17).

Europe also remains the backbone of global aid. Together, the EU and its member states provided €82.5 billion in 2025, about 48% of all aid worldwide. Its Global Gateway investment strategy has mobilised more than €306 billion, including undersea data cables linking Europe, Africa, and India.

  • €82.5 billion 82.5 billion euros EU and member-state aid in 2025, about 48% of the world total
  • 0.44% of national income. The EU’s promise is 0.7%.
  • €306 billion+ mobilised by Global Gateway from 2021 to 2025

Politically, the spotlight has dimmed. SDSN’s Europe Sustainable Development Report 2026 finds that SDG progress in Europe has stalled. It also notes that references to the SDGs have “largely disappeared” from the European Commission’s work programmes. Some Green Deal rules have been diluted, including the corporate sustainability reporting and due-diligence rules cut back in 2026. Collective EU aid stands at 0.44% of national income, well short of the 0.7% the EU has promised. SDSN is asking the EU to present a second Voluntary Review at the UN by 2027.

Dig deeper: Eurostat’s outcomes and SDSN’s political reading
  • Eurostat’s 10th monitoring report, 3 June 2026, uses 102 indicators over the short term of about five to six years. Significant progress: SDGs 8, 12, 10, 5, and 4. Nine other goals made moderate progress, and SDGs 9 and 2 did best among those. No progress: SDG 17. Moving away: SDG 15 and SDG 6.
  • 60.4% of EU adults had at least basic digital skills in 2025, up from 55.6% in 2023. Women were at 59.0% and men at 61.8%. The Digital Decade target is 80% by 2030.
  • Eurostat says sustainable development remains embedded in the Commission’s priorities for 2024 to 2029. SDSN’s Europe report of 25 February 2026 says that since 2025 those references have largely disappeared from Commission work programmes, and that the current political guidelines do not mention them. Both can be true: many outcomes are still improving, and the political spotlight has faded.
  • Only Norway, Luxembourg, Sweden, and Denmark meet the 0.7% aid target, SDSN reports. Global Gateway’s digital flagships, 12% of the total, include the EU–Africa–India Blue Raman cable.
  • From March 2026, EU sustainability reporting applies to companies with more than 1,000 employees and €450 million in turnover. Due-diligence rules apply to companies with more than 5,000 employees and €1.5 billion in turnover, with compliance from July 2029.
  • The EU’s only Voluntary Review at the UN so far was in 2023. SDSN wants a second one by 2027.

Where countries stand

Rankings flatten a lot of nuance, but they show the spread. Finland tops the SDSN SDG Index for 2026, followed by Sweden and Denmark. Even these leaders score badly on climate, biodiversity, and consumption. South Sudan is last of 169 countries, a reminder that war undoes development faster than anything else.

The fastest risers are in East and South Asia. India has climbed 18 places since 2015. Croatia ranks 13th in the world, ahead of Spain, the Netherlands, and Japan. That is something to be proud of. National data from the Croatian Bureau of Statistics shows a mixed picture underneath the rank.

Improving

Employment rose from 66.9% to 74.4% between 2020 and 2025. The gender employment gap fell from 11.2 to 5.0 percentage points. High-speed internet now reaches 79% of households, up from 43%.

Stuck

About one in five people are still at risk of poverty or social exclusion. The share of renewable energy has slipped, and net greenhouse-gas emissions have been rising since 2019.

The surprise

Only 63% of adults have basic digital skills, the same share as in 2021. Croatia is above the EU average, and far from the EU’s 80% target.

Dig deeper: Croatia is 13th globally, and 19th in the Europe-only index

These are different rankings. On the global SDG Index, Croatia is 13th with a score of 81.77, just above Latvia and Slovenia. Connection is not the same as inclusion

For a tech-for-good community, the 2026 data is both encouraging and a warning.

Encouraging

  • Account ownership has reached 79% of adults worldwide, driven largely by mobile and card accounts.
  • Sending money home digitally costs 4.59% on average, compared with 7.3% through non-digital channels.
  • The UN’s SDG database has grown from about 330,000 data points in 2016 to 3.2 million today.

The warning

  • Two in three people in the world’s least developed countries are still offline.
  • 5G reaches 84% of people in rich countries and 4% in poor ones.
  • 280 million more men than women are online.
  • As aid shrinks, national statistics offices are losing funding. In sub-Saharan Africa, only 15% of countries have a fully funded statistical plan.

That is where AI comes in. The UN report warns that when official data is missing, AI systems “fill gaps through extrapolation, interpolation or synthetic generation.” The numbers that come out can look authoritative while hiding how uncertain they are. Communities that were never counted could become even more invisible, just in a more convincing way.

Countries with strong sovereignty will shape AI to serve the public interest, while those without it risk becoming consumers of systems built for someone else’s priorities.

The Sustainable Development Goals Report 2026, United Nations

The fix is not less technology. It is technology built on trusted, locally owned data, like Sierra Leone’s river testers, Ghana’s digital-skills centres (almost 20,000 people trained, two-thirds of them women), and open digital public goods that any country can adapt.

Dig deeper: the connectivity gap, and the data Sierra Leone actually built
  • The SDG database grew from 115 indicators and about 330,000 records in 2016 to 233 indicators and 3.2 million records in 2026. Every indicator now has an agreed methodology. In the early years, about 40% did not. Country coverage rose from roughly one third of indicators to almost 70%.
  • 74% of the world was online in 2025, but only 34% in least developed countries and 38% in landlocked developing countries. 5G covers 55% of the world. 12% of people in least developed countries have no mobile broadband signal at all.
  • 77% of men and 71% of women are online. Account ownership rose from 74% in 2021 to 79% in 2025.
  • Sending $200 cost 6.36% on average in the third quarter of 2025. Digital transfers cost 4.59%, digital-only operators 3.54%, and non-digital transfers 7.3%. The target is 3%.
  • In 2025, 135 countries were implementing national statistical plans, and only 59% of those plans were fully funded. In sub-Saharan Africa, 15% were.
  • The same UN chapter warns that AI can make it possible to re-identify people even from aggregate statistics.
  • Sierra Leone’s National Water Resources Management Agency runs the monitoring programme. Since 2021 its citizen-science project has been a blueprint for six African countries. In Taia-Pampana, citizens assess heavy-metal pollution from artisanal mining. Sierra Leone and Zambia included citizen data in their 2023 submissions for indicator 6.3.2. The account comes from UNEP / GEMS/Water, in the UN Success Stories annex.
  • Ghana’s ITU–Cisco digital centre has also trained 220 community instructors and 450 people with visual impairments. The Dominican Republic’s centre has trained more than 5,000 people since 2020.
  • The 2026 High-level Political Forum declaration calls for digital public infrastructure and responsible data governance under SDG 9. In March 2026 the UN Statistical Commission endorsed an implementation strategy for the Medellín Framework for Action on Data for Sustainable Development. The 6th UN World Data Forum meets in Riyadh on 9–12 November 2026.

What comes next

In September 2027, world leaders will meet at an SDG Summit to review all 17 goals and begin negotiating what follows 2030. Public support for keeping a shared framework is strong. In SDSN’s 2026 survey across 127 countries, most respondents wanted it to continue.

Guided by the data in this report, our vision of the 2030 Agenda remains within reach.

António Guterres, UN Secretary-General, July 2026

The SDGs are off track, but they are not over. The last four years will be won the way Sierra Leone learned to see its rivers: by measuring honestly, funding what works, and making sure the people being counted are part of the counting.
